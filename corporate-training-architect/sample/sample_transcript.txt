Okay so, um, let me just talk through how I actually run client onboarding at my agency, because I think this is the thing that if I got hit by a bus tomorrow nobody could replicate. Let's call it, I don't know, the Signal-to-Contract framework. That's kind of what I call it internally anyway.

So the core idea, the thesis if you want to call it that, is this: most agencies lose deals not because the client didn't like the work, but because nobody controlled the signal-to-noise ratio in the first thirty days of contact. Every touchpoint either raises the client's confidence that you're going to solve their problem, or it raises noise — doubt, confusion, friction. My whole framework is about maximizing signal and killing noise at every single touchpoint, deliberately, on purpose, not by accident.

Alright so let me break down the actual concepts here.

First concept: the Discovery Call is not a sales call, it's a diagnostic. People mess this up constantly, new hires especially, they treat the discovery call like they're pitching. Wrong. The whole point of discovery is to extract the client's actual problem, which ninety percent of the time is NOT the problem they think they have. Like, someone comes to us saying "I need a new website," but the actual problem is their sales team can't close because there's no proof of ROI anywhere in their materials. If you pitch a website in that call you've already lost, because you're solving the surface problem instead of the root problem.

Second concept, and this is huge: the Trust Ledger. I think about every single interaction with a prospect or client as either a deposit or a withdrawal into a trust ledger. Show up late to a call, that's a withdrawal. Send a follow-up recap within two hours that's specific to what they said, not generic, that's a deposit. Miss a deadline by even a day without proactively communicating, massive withdrawal — bigger than people think, because silence around a missed deadline reads as either incompetence or dishonesty to the client, and both are fatal. The reason deals fall apart in weeks two and three of an engagement, almost always, is an accumulation of small withdrawals nobody tracked.

Third concept: Scope Ghosts. These are the unstated assumptions the client is carrying that you don't know about until they blow up the relationship. Like the client assumes "revisions" means unlimited revisions forever, you assume it means two rounds. Nobody said the wrong thing, there was just an assumption gap, a ghost in the scope. My rule is you have to actively hunt scope ghosts in the proposal stage by asking questions that surface assumptions, not just requirements.

Now let me walk through the actual process, step by step, because this is the meat of it.

Step one is the Intake Trigger. This is when a lead comes in, from whatever channel — referral, inbound form, cold outreach reply, doesn't matter. Input is basically: contact info and whatever initial context they gave you. Output of this step is a qualified or disqualified lead, plus a discovery call booked if qualified. The common mistake here is skipping qualification because you're excited about the lead — you end up doing hour-long discovery calls with people who were never going to buy.

Step two is the Discovery Call itself, which I already talked about the philosophy of. The input is the qualified lead. The output is what I call a Problem Statement Draft — a one paragraph articulation of the client's actual root problem, in their language, that you're going to mirror back to them. Common mistake: talking more than you listen. I tell people the ratio should be twenty-eighty, you talking twenty percent, them talking eighty. Second common mistake: not asking about their internal politics, like who actually signs off, because that's a scope ghost waiting to happen later.

Step three, Proposal Construction. Input is the Problem Statement Draft plus whatever budget signals you got. Output is the actual proposal document. This is where you hunt scope ghosts — I literally have a checklist of "assumption categories" I go through: timeline assumptions, revision assumptions, communication cadence assumptions, who-owns-what assumptions. Common mistake, and this one costs people real money: pricing based on your own effort estimate instead of pricing based on the value of solving the root problem you identified in discovery.

Step four is the Trust Ledger Kickoff. Once they sign, input is the signed contract, output is a kickoff call plus a written communication cadence agreement — like, we will update you every Friday by 3pm regardless of whether there's news, because silence is a withdrawal even when things are going fine. Common mistake: treating the kickoff call as a formality instead of the first real deposit opportunity of the engagement — this is where you set the tone for the entire relationship.

Now some heuristics, just rules of thumb I use constantly. One: if you're not sure whether something is a withdrawal, assume it is and communicate about it anyway — over-communication is never a withdrawal, under-communication almost always is. Two: any time a client says a soft word like "eventually" or "at some point" or "ideally," that's a scope ghost hiding in plain sight, dig into it right then. Three: the first missed deadline in a relationship is disproportionately damaging compared to later ones, because it's the first real test of whether your words match your actions — protect the first deadline at all costs, even if it means cutting scope. Four: if a discovery call is going long because you're doing a lot of talking, that's a signal you've slipped into pitch mode, stop and ask a question.

Vocabulary-wise, some terms I use with the team constantly: "signal" meaning any interaction that increases client confidence, "noise" meaning any interaction that increases client doubt or friction, "trust ledger" as I described, "scope ghost" for an unstated assumption, and "root problem" versus "surface problem" for the diagnostic distinction in discovery.

I think that's basically the whole framework. It applies from first contact all the way through the first month of delivery, and honestly once people internalize the trust ledger concept especially, everything else kind of falls into place because they start auditing their own behavior for deposits and withdrawals automatically.
